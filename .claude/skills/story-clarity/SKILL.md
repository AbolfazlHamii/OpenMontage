# Story clarity: one listen, no confusion

A viewer hears each line once, at narration speed, often on a phone, often half-watching. They cannot
re-read. If they lose the thread for 10 seconds they leave. Clarity is not dumbing down: the story can be
clever; the telling must be easy.

Owner feedback that created this skill (2026-11-14): scripts that felt "strange", that the audience "doesn't
get", "can't relate to" or found "hard". Treat every one of those words as a bug report.

## The five questions every scene must answer within its first two lines

1. **Who** is on screen (and is it someone we already know? say the name again).
2. **Where** are we (one concrete place: "the roof of the bus", not "outside").
3. **What do they want right now** (a small, physical goal: reach the pharmacy, find water, get Pip's radio).
4. **What stands in the way** (the dead, the clock, a locked door, another person).
5. **What happens if they fail** (the cost, in one short sentence).

If a scene cannot answer all five, it is not a scene yet: merge it or cut it.

## Rules

- **One new idea per sentence.** Never introduce a rule and a character in the same sentence.
- **One new concept at a time.** Introduce a new rule only after the previous one has been used on screen.
  Max one new rule per chapter.
- **Rules follow show, tell, test.** Show the rule happening (the dead turn east when a plane passes), say it
  in one plain sentence ("Rule one: they walk towards the loudest sound"), then put the hero in danger
  because of it. A rule that is never tested is forgotten; a rule that is tested is understood.
- **Same thing, same name, every time.** If it is "the airlift", never call it "the evacuation", "the
  flights" and "the operation" in other lines. Characters: always the name, never "the pilot" in one line
  and "Vera" in the next until both are tied together on screen.
- **Re-plant before you pay off.** Anything set up more than ~3 minutes earlier gets a one-line reminder
  just before it matters ("Pip's radio. The one that only reaches 300 metres.").
- **Numbers must mean something.** Every number gets a comparison or a consequence: "11 km. That's three
  hours on foot, if nothing goes wrong." Never stack more than two numbers in one sentence.
- **Geography before action.** Before any chase or set piece, one line and one wide shot that say where
  everything is: hero here, threat there, exit there. Then the action. Viewers can't feel suspense about a
  space they can't picture.
- **Time is always clear.** Every chapter opens with where we are on the clock (card or line). Flash-forwards
  and flashbacks are announced ("Eleven hours earlier.") and returned from ("Back on the runway.").
- **Cause and effect, never "and then".** Each beat is caused by the last: "but", "so", "because". A beat with
  no cause feels random; random feels fake.
- **Plain words.** Spoken English for a 12-year-old; no jargon, no abbreviations without a picture. In Persian:
  spoken Tehrani, not book Persian (see `skills/creative/persian-localization.md`).
- **Short sentences, but not all short.** Mostly 6-14 words, with a 2-4 word punch after big moments.
- **Pronouns only when the reference is unmistakable.** With two people on screen, use names.
- **The picture carries what the words don't.** If the line says "the fence breaks", the shot shows the fence
  breaking. Never narrate something the viewer must imagine while the screen shows something else.
- **Twists must be fair.** A twist works only if the clue was planted where the viewer could see it.
  Write the clue line and the twist line next to each other in the loops table.

## The cold-viewer audit (run on every draft)

Pretend you are a viewer who has never seen the channel, listening once, on a phone. For each line, ask:

| Mark | Meaning | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| `?WHO` | I don't know who this is or forgot them | name + one identifying detail |
| `?WHERE` | I can't picture the place | one concrete location word + establishing shot |
| `?WHY` | I don't know why they do this | add the want or the cause before the action |
| `?RULE` | A rule is used before it was shown or after it was forgotten | show/tell/test it, or re-plant |
| `?WORD` | A word I'd have to look up or a term used before it's explained | plain word |
| `?NUM` | Numbers I can't hold | one number, with a comparison |
| `?JUMP` | Time or place jumped without a signpost | card or "Two hours later." |
| `?SO-WHAT` | I don't care what happens here | add stakes for a person we like |
| `?SAME` | Feels like something we already saw | cut, or change the obstacle |

Write the audit as a table (line number, mark, fix) next to the script, fix every mark, then audit again. A
draft is clear when a full pass finds no `?WHO`, `?WHY`, `?RULE` or `?JUMP` marks.

## Quick tests

- **Summary test:** after each chapter, can the story so far be told in two sentences? If not, it's too
  complicated.
- **Read-aloud test:** read it at narration speed. Any place you stumble, the TTS will too, and the viewer
  will lose the thread.
- **Mute test (for the shot list):** with the sound off, does the picture alone show who, where and what's
  happening? If not, add the establishing shot or the insert.
- **Explain-it-to-a-friend test:** the premise must fit in one sentence a friend would repeat: "The dead walk
  towards the airport every time a plane lands, and you have to get there before the last one."

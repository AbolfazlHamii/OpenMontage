# Human story: make people care

Viewers don't stay for the zombies. They stay for a person they like who is in trouble. Every rule below
serves one goal: the viewer should feel something specific at least once a minute (worry, relief, a laugh,
a lump in the throat) and should want one particular person to be okay.

## Likable people (in the first 60 seconds of them)

Give every main character, early, at least two of:
- **Kindness to someone weaker** (Milo shares his last bottle with the dog; Vera came back for a stranger).
- **Competence** at one specific thing (Theo knows every shortcut; Vera can land anywhere).
- **Humour** under pressure (a dry line, not a joke-joke).
- **A wound or a fear** shown, not told (Pip only talks to people in yellow shirts).
- **A want the viewer shares** (get home, protect someone, keep a promise).

Villains and rivals (Grant) get a reason the viewer can understand, even if they hate it: "He counts seats
twice because last time he counted once."

## Specific beats generic

- One small true detail beats a paragraph: Pip draws everything she hears; Theo makes the sound of the till
  on the toy radio. Find one such detail per character per episode and reuse it as a callback.
- Concrete senses in second person: what "you" feel in the body. "Your hands smell of petrol." "The
  radio is warm from your pocket." Not "you feel scared".
- Use objects to carry emotion: the toy radio, the drawing, the hat. A prop that returns at the end hits
  harder than any line.

## Emotion without melodrama

- **Show the reaction, not the label.** Never "it was heartbreaking". Show the hand that stops, the line that
  isn't finished, the silence.
- **Underplay the biggest moment.** The saddest line is the shortest and the plainest: "You stay."
- **Set up, then pay off.** A sacrifice works only if the cost was planted (Vera's leg, her one rule, the
  last flare) and the relationship had a warm moment first. Plant warmth 2-5 minutes before you break it.
- **Give the audience a breath after every peak.** After a chase: a quiet beat (sharing food, a joke, a
  look). Tension that never releases becomes numb.
- **Hold silence.** On the 2-3 biggest beats per episode, mark `[BEAT]` or `[HOLD 2s]` and let the music drop.
- **Hope must stay alive.** Even the darkest chapter ends with one small win or one reason to keep going.

## Dialogue that sounds like people

- Short. People speak in fragments, interrupt, and don't explain what both of them already know.
- Subtext: say less than they mean. "Someone has to play the announcement." means "I'm staying to save you."
- Each character sounds different: Pip literal and blunt, Vera clipped and practical, Grant formal and
  counting, Milo (when he speaks) quiet and wry.
- Never use dialogue to deliver exposition both characters know ("As you know, the planes land every 40
  minutes").
- One line of dialogue per character per scene is often enough; let the narrator carry the rest.

## Humour and warmth

- One light moment per chapter, from character, not from jokes: the dog steals the splint; Pip rates your
  plan "four out of ten".
- Warmth is small and physical: a hand on a shoulder, sharing the last cinnamon bun, a nickname.

## The second-person "you"

- "You" is the hero, so "you" must be likable too: make choices the viewer would be proud of, and one
  mistake they'd forgive.
- Put the viewer inside choices: "You could run. You don't." Ask a comment question only after a real
  dilemma: "Would you have gone back for her?"

## Avoid robotic, AI-sounding writing

Cut on sight:
- "little did you know", "it was a testament to", "in a world where", "the stakes had never been higher",
  "a chill ran down your spine", "every second counted", "against all odds", "unbeknownst".
- Lists of three adjectives; every sentence the same length; rhetorical questions in a row.
- Explaining the emotion after showing it.
- Perfect symmetry and neat morals at the end. Real endings leave something unsaid.
- Over-describing ("the dimly lit, eerily quiet, abandoned street"): pick the one detail that matters.

## Persian version

Write the Persian as a retelling for a Persian listener, not a translation: spoken Tehrani, Persian rhythm,
Persian idiom for jokes and endearments (not literal English jokes). Keep names, numbers and story beats the
same so the shots still match. See `skills/creative/persian-localization.md`.

## Human-ness check (before the owner sees a draft)

For each chapter, write one line each:
1. Who do I want the viewer to worry about here, and why do they like them?
2. What does the viewer feel at the end of the chapter (one word)?
3. Which small, specific detail is in this chapter that nobody else would write?
4. Where is the breath after the peak?
If any answer is empty or generic, rewrite the chapter before moving on.
